**Authors:** Ashley J. Thomas and Min Feldman, Harvard University
**Hosting platform:** Children Helping Science (CHS)

## Background

When people explain someone's behavior, they can frame it as a property of the individual (e.g., "Rowan likes to play with bugs") or in generic terms (e.g., "Wugs like to play with bugs"; "People like to play with bugs"). In Study 1 (N = 127, ages 5–8), children — especially 7–8-year-olds — chose the individual-level explainer as a target's best friend above chance, but also chose them as the target's boss. In Study 2 (N = 108, ages 5–8), which forced a trade-off by having children assign the best-friend and boss roles simultaneously, older children (7–8) assigned the individual-level explainer to the best-friend role above chance (M = .64, BF+0 > 100) while younger children responded at chance. In Study 3 (ages 6–8; data collection ongoing under a registered extension at the time of this registration), the group-kind foil ("Wugs") was replaced with a generic-person foil ("People"), and older children's preference was greatly reduced (at the registered stopping point: M = .56, BF+0 = 0.59, inconclusive; including pre-registration data, M = .52, BF+0 = 0.23).

Children's open-ended explanations in Study 3 suggested a specific mechanism for this reduction: many children parsed the individual-level explanation as evidence that the speaker *knows* the target, but split on the relational meaning of knowing — some mapped knowledge to friendship ("she sounds like she knows him") and others to authority ("if Cora was Arlo's boss he would know that Arlo likes to sniff things"). Because knowledge is part of some children's concept of a boss, the best-friend/boss trade-off may measure children's folk theories of bosses in addition to their inferences about closeness.

Study 4 addresses this with two changes. First, the two roles are now **loves** versus **likes** — both positive, both compatible with the familiarity established in the character introductions, and differing only in intimacy. Neither role implies privileged knowledge, so the knowledge reading of individuation cannot be routed to a non-intimate role. The forced-choice (ipsative) structure is retained: a general preference for the individuating speaker cannot produce the predicted pattern, because one speaker must receive each role. Second, each child answers a direct folk-theory probe at the end of the session ("Who loves someone: their boss, their best friend, or both of them?", with a follow-up "Who loves them more?" for children who answer "both"), characterizing children's beliefs about which of the relationships used in Studies 1–3 involve love — and therefore how cleanly the loves role maps onto the best-friend role of the earlier designs.

**Hypotheses.** H1 (individuation-as-intimacy): children — particularly 7–8-year-olds — assign the individual-level explainer to the *loves* role above chance. H2 (development): the effect is stronger in older (7–8) than younger (6) children. H3 (epistemic): based on Studies 1–3 we expect no effect of the epistemic frame ("Hmm…must" vs. "Yes…"). If children's reduced preference in Study 3 reflected the boss role's knowledge-ambiguity (rather than the loss of the kind label), the loves/likes contrast should restore an above-chance preference in older children even with the People foil; if the kind label was necessary for the inference, children should respond at chance here as well.

## Foreknowledge of data

No data exist for Study 4. This registration precedes the first participant. The design, outcome coding, models, priors, and decision rules follow the Study 2 and Study 3 registrations, fixed before any Study 4 data existed.

## Study design

A 2-level within-subjects design over 4 test trials: two blocks of 2 trials, one block per epistemic frame (Hmm / Yes), with the epistemic frame held constant within a block. Every trial asks the child to assign both the *loves* and *likes* roles simultaneously by choosing between two complete assignments.

On each trial: (1) the child meets the target and two speakers, hears that the target has known both speakers for several years and spent equal time with both, and that one of them loves the target and one of them likes the target ("You'll have to figure out which is which!"); (2) the child sees the target's unusual behavior; (3) the framing is repeated as a reminder, then the two speakers explain the behavior — one individual-level (e.g., "Rowan likes to play with bugs") and one people-level (e.g., "People like to play with bugs"); which speaker individuates is randomized per trial; (4) the child chooses between two option cards showing the two possible role assignments. The two speakers appear in fixed left-right positions on both option cards (only the role labels swap), and the option audio names the speakers in the same left-right order as displayed. The role-to-speaker mapping is counterbalanced across trials. Block order (Hmm-first vs. Yes-first) is randomized across participants. Four target characters per child are drawn without replacement from a pool of 11, randomized per participant.

**Attention check (new).** On every trial, after the two speeches and BEFORE the choice options appear (same screen), one of the two speeches is replayed and the child is asked "Who said that?", choosing between the two speakers' portraits. Which statement is probed (individual-level vs. people-level) is counterbalanced within child: one of each per block, order randomized, so each child receives two probes of each type. Because the probe re-exposes one statement immediately before the choice, probe type is included as a counterbalanced nuisance variable (see exploratory analyses); re-exposure is equated across statement types within child. If the child answers incorrectly, the probed speech is replayed once and the question is asked again; if the second answer is also incorrect, the correct speaker is highlighted while a correction clip plays ("Good try! It was actually this one who said that!"). The child's FIRST answer is the recorded attention check. The check yields memCheckProbe (individual | group), memCheckCorrect (0/1, first answer), and memCheckCorrect2 (0/1 second answer after the replay; blank when the first answer was correct) per trial. The replay and correction keep children oriented but add exposure only on error trials; because they occur before the choice and only following an incorrect first answer, memCheckCorrect is included in the registered sensitivity analysis exactly as specified below.

**Folk-theory probe (new).** After the final trial, the child is asked "Who loves someone: their boss, their best friend, or both of them?" (boss/best-friend button order randomized; "both of them" always last), yielding lovesWhoAnswer (boss | best_friend | both). Children who answer "both" receive one follow-up, "Who loves them more?" (two buttons, order randomized), yielding lovesMoreAnswer (boss | best_friend; blank for children not asked).

## Manipulated variables

Epistemic frame of the speeches (within-subjects, blocked): "Yes, X likes to [behavior]" (knowledgeable) vs. "Hmm, X must like to [behavior]" (apparently inferring). Independently, one speaker always explains the behavior at the level of the individual (using the target's name) and the other at the level of a generic person ("People").

## Sampling and participants

Target sample: children ages 6 to 8 years, recruited through Children Helping Science.

Sample size: target N = 75 (25 per year of age: 6, 7, 8), with the goal of at least 60 retained after exclusions (20 per age), matching Studies 2–3.

Stopping rule: recruit until at least 20 children are retained per year of age (6, 7, 8 ≥ 20 each, after exclusions). If recruitment reaches 100 total sessions and the target is not met for one age, we will stop and report sample sizes as obtained. Because we use Bayesian statistics, which can accumulate evidence for the null, we may transparently extend data collection if the key posteriors remain inconclusive at the stopping point; any extension will be announced on this registration before additional analysis, with an explicit exit rule (as was done for Study 3).

Inclusion criteria: caregiver-reported age 6.00–8.99 years on the date of the session (exact age additionally computed from the CHS birthdate for continuous-age analyses; implausible conflicts resolved toward the more plausible value and reported); caregiver consented to participate and to data sharing; child completed at least one experimental trial; session began after this registration's timestamp.

Exclusion criteria: duplicate sessions for the same participant (keep the first complete or partial session); sessions without a verifiable CHS consent record (e.g., siblings participating on another child's link); sessions flagged as fraudulent or invalid on consent-video review; sessions in which the child was clearly distracted, prompted by the caregiver, or otherwise non-compliant per video review. Whole sessions are excluded; individual-trial exclusions are not used. The attention check is NOT used to exclude sessions; it feeds the registered sensitivity analysis below. Participant loss is reported at each step.

## Measured variables and derived indices

Primary derived variable — choseIndividualAsLoves (trial level, binary): 1 if the selected assignment places the individual-level explainer in the *loves* role (and therefore the people-level explainer in the *likes* role); 0 otherwise. Computed automatically by the experiment software.

Per-participant proportions: for each participant and epistemic cell (Hmm, Yes), p = mean of choseIndividualAsLoves across that participant's trials in that cell (2 per cell; fewer if incomplete). These are the inputs to the chance tests.

Attention check: memCheckCorrect (0/1 per trial); per-participant accuracy = mean across completed trials.

Folk-theory probe: lovesWhoAnswer (boss | best_friend | both) and, for children answering "both," lovesMoreAnswer (boss | best_friend); one probe row per child. Derived index lovesBestFriendOnly (binary): 1 if lovesWhoAnswer = best_friend, or lovesWhoAnswer = both with lovesMoreAnswer = best_friend; 0 otherwise.

Exact age: age_years = (session date − birthdate)/365.25 from the CHS birthdate; caregiver-reported age + 0.5 where unavailable or implausible. Centered for continuous-age models. Age group: Younger = 6.00–6.99; Older = 7.00–8.99.

Log response time (exploratory): log_rt = ln(rt_ms), excluding trials < 500 ms or > 90,000 ms.

## Analysis plan

All analyses in R using brms (Bürkner, 2017) and BayesFactor. Code committed to the project repository. Priors, coding, and decision rules are identical to Studies 2–3: Normal(0, 1.5) on intercepts, Normal(0, 1) on sum-coded (+1/−1) effects, Exponential(2) on random-effect SDs; 4 chains, 4000 iterations (1000 warmup), adapt_delta = .95. For each effect we report the 95% credible interval (log-odds and probability scales), the posterior probability of the predicted direction (support threshold .95 with CrI excluding zero), and the Bayes factor against the model with that term removed (BF10 ≥ 3 / ≥ 10 moderate/strong; ≤ 1/3 evidence for the null).

Primary confirmatory model:
brm(choseIndividualAsLoves ~ epistemic * ageGroup + (1 | participantId), family = bernoulli(), ...)
H1 is tested by the intercept (grand-mean log-odds > 0 under sum coding) and the cell-level directional chance tests (BayesFactor::ttestBF on per-participant proportions, mu = 0.5, nullInterval = c(0, Inf)), reported overall and stratified by age group. H2 by the ageGroup coefficient and a continuous-age refit. H3 by the epistemic terms.

Confirmatory cross-study comparisons. (a) Study 4 vs. Study 3 (both People foil): brm(DV ~ roles * ageGroup + (1 | participantId)) on the combined confirmatory samples, roles sum-coded (loves/likes = +1, best-friend/boss = −1), where each study's DV is "individual-level explainer assigned to the more intimate role." The knowledge-routing account predicts a positive roles effect in older children (loves/likes > best-friend/boss with the People foil); the kind-label-necessity account predicts no roles effect (both at chance). (b) Study 4 vs. Study 2: analogous model with foil+roles differing; reported to situate effect sizes (exploratory emphasis given two differences between the designs).

Registered moderator analysis (folk-theory probe). We report the distribution of lovesWhoAnswer (and lovesMoreAnswer among "both" responders) overall and by age group, characterizing how prevalent the belief is that love is specific to intimate relationships rather than shared with authority relationships. As a registered exploratory analysis, we refit the primary model adding lovesBestFriendOnly (yes = +1, no = −1) and its interactions with the intercept-level effects. If the loves role carries intimacy for essentially all children (as intended by the design), the moderator should be near ceiling and uninformative; if a substantial minority extends love to bosses, this analysis tests whether the individuation-to-loves mapping is specific to children who reserve love for intimate relations. Because Study 3 did not include this probe, cross-study comparison on it is descriptive only.

Sensitivity analyses: continuous age; tighter/wider priors (Normal(0, .5) / Normal(0, 2.5)); complete-sessions-only; and attention-check sensitivity — refit the primary model restricted to children with memCheckCorrect ≥ 3 of 4 (or ≥ 75% of completed trials), reporting both.

Exploratory (not confirmatory): response times; item (target-character) random intercepts; counterbalancing nuisance variables (rolesSwapped, block order, attention-check probe type — including whether choices tilt toward the just-replayed speaker) as fixed effects; trial-order effects; qualitative coding of the open-ended "why?" answers using the scheme developed from Study 3 (knowledge→intimacy, knowledge→authority, non-semantic strategy; finalized before coding); attention-check accuracy by age; probe answer by age.

## Transformations

As in Studies 2–3: trial-level card choice recoded to choseIndividualAsLoves by the software at response time; exact age from CHS birthdate (fallback reported age + .5), binned 6.00–6.99 / 7.00–8.99 or centered continuous; all categorical predictors sum-coded; per-participant proportions for chance tests; RT trimming (<500 ms, >90,000 ms) and log transform for exploratory RT analyses.

## Missing data

Missing trials are ignored: hierarchical models and per-participant proportions use all available trials; the complete-sessions-only sensitivity analysis checks robustness. A missing probe answer (session ended early) excludes the child from the moderator analysis only.
